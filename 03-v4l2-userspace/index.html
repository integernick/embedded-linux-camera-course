Lesson 02 ended with a camera that probed and a /dev/video0. This lesson uses that node. It captures raw frames with two generic Linux tools, and then it reads one frame byte by byte. No NVIDIA camera service takes part, so every sample in the file is a value that the sensor sent.

Section 1 first draws the picture that the rest assumes. It names the three blocks of any camera pipeline, on a Raspberry Pi as on a Jetson, and the kernel objects that stand for them. Then three topics come up, and every camera bring-up meets them. They are the kernel objects behind the device nodes, the source of the mode list, and the meaning of the bytes in a raw frame. The nine sections take them in that order. Each section shows its commands together with their output, so the lesson can be read without a board.

The text describes two boards. The Jetson Orin Nano runs JetPack 7.2.1 (Jetson Linux r39.2.1), with kernel 6.8 in the kernel-noble tree. Each section follows the Jetson first, and its last part shows the same step on the Raspberry Pi 5.

What this lesson uses

The stock camera is the Raspberry Pi High Quality Camera with its Sony IMX477 sensor, enabled by the lab of Lesson 02. NVIDIA's driver nv_imx477 probes it. Every board transcript of this lesson shows that camera.

The two tools are media-ctl and v4l2-ctl, both from the v4l-utils package. media-ctl prints how the parts of the camera connect. v4l2-ctl lists the formats, sets the controls, and writes frames to a file. The toy file is a raw frame of 8×4 pixels that a script of this lesson writes, examples/make_toy_raw.py. It has the layout of a real Jetson capture. Sections 5 and 6 read it byte by byte, before the lab reads a real capture of 8 million pixels.

The Raspberry Pi side is read from two drivers of the Raspberry Pi kernel. They are imx477.c, the sensor driver of Lesson 01, and rp1-cfe, the driver of the camera front end. Its transcripts are searches in those sources, because this course has no board capture of a Raspberry Pi yet.

1. What a camera is made of, on any SoC

Lesson 02 ended with a camera that probed and a /dev/video0. Before that node is used, one question comes first: what is behind it. The answer is the same on every Linux system with a camera connector. So this section describes it without the Jetson, and section 2 returns to the devkit.

A camera is never one device. Between the lens and the memory of the SoC sit three jobs, and each job is a separate piece of hardware:

Job Hardware Raspberry Pi 4 Raspberry Pi 5 Jetson Orin
Produce pixels The sensor on the camera module, configured over I2C, emitting over CSI-2 IMX477 IMX477 IMX477
Receive the CSI-2 lanes A block inside the SoC that terminates the lanes and unpacks the packets Unicam CSI-2 receiver of the camera front end, in the RP1 chip NVCSI
Write pixels to memory A block with a DMA engine that stores each line into a buffer Unicam, the same block DMA engines of the same camera front end VI

The sensor is on the module and talks to the SoC over two separate connections. Its registers are written over I2C, the bus of Lesson 01. Its pixels leave over MIPI CSI-2, a one-way high-speed link with its own pins (Lesson 06). The two blocks that follow are not on the module. They are parts of the SoC, each with a register window at a fixed address. The pixel stream passes between them inside the chip (Lesson 02, section 6).

On a Raspberry Pi 4 one block does both of the last two jobs. On a Raspberry Pi 5 they are two parts of one block, the camera front end of RP1. On a Jetson they are two blocks. That is the only difference between the columns, and it decides how many drivers the kernel needs.

The three jobs of a camera pipeline, with the block of each job and its name on three boards

One driver per block, and what a driver is

The kernel has one driver per block. A driver is not a program and not a thread. It is a set of functions that the kernel calls at defined moments.

probe runs once, when the device appears (Lesson 01). Later, each system call that a program makes on a node of the driver runs one of its functions. An ioctl on /dev/video0 runs inside the program that made it. The driver only runs when something calls it.

The sensor driver is an I2C driver, because its device is a child of an I2C adapter. The receiver and the writer are blocks of the SoC, so they are not on any bus that the kernel can scan. The kernel calls such a block a platform device. Its device tree node carries the register address in reg, as nvcsi@15a00000 does in Lesson 02. A platform driver probes it by its compatible string. The matching rule is the one of Lesson 01, on a different bus.

The kernel objects, and the words of the kernel documentation

V4L2 gives each job a kernel object. The block that writes to memory owns a video_device. That is the object behind /dev/video0, and frames leave the kernel through it only. Every block before it is a v4l2_subdev: an object with a table of functions that the other drivers call. The kernel documentation introduces it in four lines:

container$ TREE=/build/work-l4t-r39.2.1/Linux_for_Tegra/source
container$ sed -n '6,9p' "$TREE/kernel/kernel-noble/Documentation/driver-api/media/v4l2-subdev.rst"
Many drivers need to communicate with sub-devices. These devices can do all
sort of tasks, but most commonly they handle audio and/or video muxing,
encoding or decoding. For webcams common sub-devices are sensors and camera
controllers.

A subdev can also have a node of its own, /dev/v4l-subdevN, a side door to the controls of one block. The third object is the media controller: a map of all blocks and of the connections between them, behind /dev/media0. The map is built from the ports and endpoint nodes of the device tree (Lesson 02, section 6). So the device tree draws the wiring once, and the kernel reads it twice: to probe the drivers, and to connect their objects.

How the drivers find each other

The three drivers probe in no fixed order. The sensor probes when the I2C core finds its node, and the SoC blocks probe when the platform bus finds theirs. The writer's driver, the owner of the video node, therefore waits.

At probe it registers a list of the device tree endpoints that it needs. Each time a subdev with one of those nodes registers, the V4L2 core calls the bound function of the waiting driver. When the list is complete, it calls complete, and the video node appears. Section 3 shows the bound line of the Jetson in the boot log.

Source: the two callbacks of the waiting driver
Jetson Linux r39.2.1kernel/kernel-noble/include/media/v4l2-async.hlines 95–104
struct v4l2_async_notifier_operations {
    int (*bound)(struct v4l2_async_notifier *notifier,
             struct v4l2_subdev *subdev,
             struct v4l2_async_connection *asc);
    int (*complete)(struct v4l2_async_notifier *notifier);
    void (*unbind)(struct v4l2_async_notifier *notifier,
               struct v4l2_subdev *subdev,
               struct v4l2_async_connection *asc);
    void (*destroy)(struct v4l2_async_connection *asc);
};
  • The waiting driver fills this table. bound runs once per subdev that arrives, and complete runs once, when every expected subdev has arrived. Lesson 05 reads the mechanism in full.

Two kinds of programs

Above the nodes, two kinds of programs use a camera. The first kind talks to the nodes directly with the V4L2 system calls. v4l2-ctl and media-ctl are such programs, and so is this lesson. They work on every board in the table, with the same commands.

The second kind is a camera service. It runs the exposure, gain and white-balance loops (3A), drives the ISP of the SoC, and hands finished images to applications. On a Raspberry Pi that service is libcamera. On a Jetson it is Argus (section 9). Both sit on top of the same three drivers.

2. One camera, three kinds of device node

Section 1 named three blocks. On the devkit they are the IMX477, NVCSI and VI, and three drivers serve them. The sensor driver is nv_imx477, an I2C driver. It is written against tegracam, a helper library of NVIDIA that registers the subdev and the controls for every NVIDIA sensor driver (Lesson 07). The NVCSI driver and the VI driver are platform drivers. This section finds the device nodes of the three.

Lesson 02 ended with one /dev/video0. That node is the visible part of a larger set. After the boot, the camera of the devkit appears as four device nodes of three kinds:

crw-rw----+ 1 root video 81, 3 Oct  4 10:12 /dev/v4l-subdev0
crw-rw----+ 1 root video 81, 4 Oct  4 10:12 /dev/v4l-subdev1
crw-rw----+ 1 root video 81, 1 Oct  4 10:12 /dev/video0

One camera needs several nodes because a camera pipeline is not one device. The sensor is an I2C device with its own driver. NVCSI, the CSI-2 receiver of the SoC, has a second driver. VI, the block that writes pixels into memory, has a third. Each of the three drivers registers a v4l2_subdev: a kernel object that stands for one processing stage. The kernel gives a stage its own node so that a program can reach the controls of that stage directly.

Two more objects complete the picture. VI also creates the video_device, the only node that moves frames. And the media controller keeps a map of how the stages connect, which /dev/media0 shows:

/dev/video0 video_device with a videobuf2 queue Frame I/O: set a format, queue buffers, stream The VI channel driver, one per capture channel
/dev/media0 media_device Topology: entities, pads and links The media-controller registration of the VI driver
/dev/v4l-subdev* v4l2_subdev Direct access to the controls and formats of one stage The V4L2 core, one per registered subdev

The name and the number of the video node

After the bind, VI names its video node after the subdev that feeds it: vi-output, imx477 9-001a. On a board with several sensors, that name tells which sensor a given /dev/videoN captures from. The number of the node comes from the overlay instead. A devnode property on the sensor node requests it, and both the stock overlay and the course's overlay request video0. So a single sensor is /dev/video0 with either overlay.

3. The bind in the boot log, and the graph that media-ctl prints

The bind in the boot log

The three drivers probe independently. The sensor driver probes when the I2C core finds its DT node (Lesson 01). VI probes as a platform driver. They meet at one moment, which the boot log records in two lines:

illustrative, not captured on a board
[    9.312482] imx477 9-001a: tegracam sensor driver:imx477_v2.0.6
[    9.312604] tegra-camrtc-capture-vi tegra-capture-vi: subdev imx477 9-001a bound

First the sensor driver finishes its probe, and the tegracam framework prints its banner. Then VI reports that it accepted the new subdev. That second line is the bind. Before it, a probed sensor is an i2c_client with a subdev that nothing uses. After it, the subdev is part of the graph of VI, and /dev/video0 reaches that sensor. So a sensor that probes without a bound line is a sensor that /dev/video0 cannot capture from.

imx477 9-001a is the name of the subdev: the driver name, then the I2C bus and the address. Bus 9 is the first adapter of the camera mux, the one that carries the camera under a stock overlay. The lab of Lesson 02 scanned that bus with i2cdetect.

Source: where the two lines are printed

The framework prints the banner, with its own version in it:

The bound line comes from the VI driver, tegra-camrtc-capture-vi. Its V4L2-async callback runs when a subdev that the device tree names becomes available:

Three words describe the dump:

  • An entity is one processing stage: one registered subdev, or the video node itself.
  • A pad is a connection point on an entity. A sink pad receives data, and a source pad emits it.
  • A link joins a source pad to a sink pad. [ENABLED] means that data can flow over it.

The dump lists the entities in the order of their numbers, which is not the order of the data. Drawn in the order of the data, the same graph is a chain:

The graph is the device tree, as the kernel holds it

Every part of the dump comes from the device tree of Lesson 02. The sensor entity is the sensor node, named by its driver in probe with strncpy(tc_dev->name, "imx477", ...) in nv_imx477.c. tegracam appends the I2C bus and address. The NVCSI entity is the nvcsi@15a00000 node under host1x. And each link is one ports { endpoint { remote-endpoint }} chain of that overlay (Lesson 02, section 6).

So the dump shows the wiring of the device tree as the running kernel holds it. That makes it a diagnostic. A missing entity or a missing link names the faulty layer before any driver code is read.

4. Where the mode list comes from

A capture starts with a format: a pixel format, a width and a height. So the first question to a camera is which formats it supports. v4l2-ctl asks the video node:

illustrative, not captured on a board
orin$ v4l2-ctl -d /dev/video0 --list-formats-ext
ioctl: VIDIOC_ENUM_FMT
    Type: Video Capture

    [0]: 'RG10' (10-bit Bayer RGRG/GBGB)
        Size: Discrete 3840x2160
            Interval: Discrete 0.033s (30.000 fps)
        Size: Discrete 1920x1080
            Interval: Discrete 0.017s (60.000 fps)

The camera offers one pixel format, RG10, in two sizes with one frame rate each. Three ioctls produce the listing: VIDIOC_ENUM_FMT for the pixel formats, VIDIOC_ENUM_FRAMESIZES for the sizes of each format, and VIDIOC_ENUM_FRAMEINTERVALS for the rates of each size.

None of these numbers is measured from the sensor. Each one comes from a table that a person wrote, in one of two places: the C source of the driver, or the device tree. The place decides where a missing or wrong mode is fixed:

Two sources and two listings: v4l2-ctl reads the C table and the pixel format, Argus reads every mode node

Sizes and rates come from the C table

A mode is one complete setting of the sensor, stored as a table of register values. It fixes the size of the frame and the area of the sensor that is read. It also fixes the bit depth, the range of frame rates and the rate of the CSI-2 link. So a mode is more than a resolution, and two modes can have the same size.

Every tegracam sensor driver has a frmfmt table, a C array with one row per mode. The IMX477 table has two rows, so v4l2-ctl printed two sizes:

Jetson Linux r39.2.1nvidia-oot/drivers/media/i2c/imx477_mode_tbls.hlines 844–847
static const struct camera_common_frmfmt imx477_frmfmt[] = {
    {{3840, 2160}, imx477_30_fr, 1, 0, IMX477_MODE_3840x2160_30FPS},
    {{1920, 1080}, imx477_60_fr, 1, 0, IMX477_MODE_1920x1080_60FPS},
};

Each row is {size, framerate list, framerate count, hdr flag, mode index}. The last field selects the register table that set_mode writes into the sensor over I2C when that mode starts. Those register tables fill the top of the same header.

5. The bytes of a raw frame

A capture with v4l2-ctl writes the frames to a file exactly as VI wrote them to memory. Two facts describe that file. The first is the color mosaic of the sensor. The second is the storage of one sample, which section 4 found in the VI table.

A color sensor does not capture color pixels. Each photosite sits under one color filter, and the filters form a repeating 2×2 mosaic. pixel_phase = "rggb" names its order: red and green on even rows, green and blue on odd rows. That is the "RGRG.. GBGB.." string of the VI table. A cell has two green sites by design, because human vision takes most of its detail from green.

The RGGB mosaic of the sensor, and the 16-bit little-endian word that holds one 10-bit sample

Two operations turn such a mosaic into a picture. Demosaicing computes three colors for each pixel from one sample per site. White balance scales red and blue so that a white object appears white. On a Jetson both belong to the ISP, which Argus drives with its 3A loops. A v4l2-ctl capture bypasses all of it: VI writes the output of the sensor's analog-to-digital converter to memory unchanged. Lesson 12 covers the ISP side.

A toy frame, byte by byte

The layout is easier to see on a small frame than on 8 million pixels. The script examples/make_toy_raw.py writes two frames of 8×4 pixels in the layout of a Jetson capture. Every color site holds a constant, so each byte can be checked by hand:

container$ W=/tmp/lesson03 && mkdir -p "$W" && cd "$W"
container$ python3 /course/lessons/03-v4l2-userspace/examples/make_toy_raw.py toy.raw
wrote toy.raw: 2 frames of 8x4, 16-bit words, R=600 Gr=900 Gb=900 B=300, then one frame of 1023
container$ stat -c %s toy.raw
128
container$ od -An -tx1 -N 32 toy.raw
 58 02 84 03 58 02 84 03 58 02 84 03 58 02 84 03
 84 03 2c 01 84 03 2c 01 84 03 2c 01 84 03 2c 01

The file is 128 bytes: 8 × 4 pixels × 2 bytes × 2 frames, with no header and no padding. The first od line is row 0 of the first frame, and the second line is row 1. Each pair of bytes is one pixel, stored as a 16-bit word with the low byte first. So 58 02 is 0x0258 = 600, the R site, and 84 03 is 0x0384 = 900, a green site. Row 1 alternates 900 and 2c 01 = 300, the B site.

The upper six bits of every word are zero, because a 10-bit sample fills only bits 0 to 9. That is the definition of V4L2_PIX_FMT_SRGGB10 in the kernel documentation (see References), and it matches the T_R16 format of VI. RG12 has the same layout with 12 valid bits and four zero bits. So a frame is always width × height × 2 bytes, and a capture of five frames is five such frames back to back.

The same samples in a packed file on a Raspberry Pi 5

The front end of the Raspberry Pi can write the samples as they are on the CSI-2 link, without the padding to 16 bits. In that packed layout four 10-bit pixels use five bytes. The toy script writes the same two frames in it:

host$ python3 examples/make_toy_raw.py toy_packed.raw --packed10
wrote toy_packed.raw: 2 frames of 8x4, packed 10-bit, R=600 Gr=900 Gb=900 B=300, then one frame of 1023

6. Why a raw frame looks dark, gridded and green

A raw frame opened in an ordinary image viewer looks broken in three ways: almost black, covered by a fine grid, and green. Each effect is correct raw data, and the toy file shows all three in numbers. The viewer writes two PNG files for the comparison, a grayscale image of the samples and a simple RGB demosaic:

container$ python3 -c 'print(f"{900 / 65535:.1%}", 900 << 6)'
1.4% 57600
container$ python3 /course/lessons/03-v4l2-userspace/solutions/bayer_view.py toy.raw --width 8 --height 4 --bits 10 --out toy.png
wrote toy.png
container$ python3 /course/lessons/03-v4l2-userspace/solutions/bayer_view.py toy.raw --width 8 --height 4 --bits 10 --demosaic --out toy_rgb.png
wrote toy_rgb.png

First, the image looks almost black. A viewer that opens the file as 16-bit grayscale maps 65535 to white, while the brightest possible sample is 1023. So the green sites of the toy, 900, are 1.4% of white. The data sits in the low bits of each word, and a viewer expects it in the high bits. The fix is a shift left by six bits, a multiplication by 64, which turns 900 into 57600. The viewer writes its grayscale PNG with that shift applied.

Second, the image shows a fine checkerboard at high zoom. Adjacent sites sit under different filters, so even a uniform scene alternates in level from site to site. Row 0 of the toy alternates 600 and 900 in the same way. In a real capture, the 2×2 structure is visible at 800% zoom on any bright, uniform area.

Third, a simple demosaic looks green. The RGB PNG holds one pixel per 2×2 cell: R, the mean of the two greens, and B, reduced to 8 bits. Its first pixel is (150, 225, 75), that is 600 ÷ 4, 900 ÷ 4 and 300 ÷ 4, with green on top. A real sensor gives the same order for a white object, because its green sites read higher than its red and blue sites. Nothing on this path scales the channels, so the tint stays. The ISP corrects it, and this path skips the ISP.

8. Three controls with side effects

Most controls do what their names say. Three entries of the listing do more: the gain and exposure ranges, sensor_mode, and bypass_mode.

The +1 in the ranges

The device tree says max_gain_val = "356", but the listing shows max=357. The exposure range ends at 683710 instead of 683709. tegracam adds one to both maxima on purpose, when it copies the ranges from the device tree:

Jetson Linux r39.2.1nvidia-oot/drivers/media/platform/tegra/camera/tegracam_ctrls.clines 614–620
        case TEGRA_CAMERA_CID_GAIN:
            err = v4l2_ctrl_modify_range(ctrl,
                ctrlprops->min_gain_val,
                ctrlprops->max_gain_val + 1,
                ctrlprops->step_gain_val,
                ctrlprops->default_gain);
            break;

The extra value is not a gain. It is a marker with the meaning unknown, and a write of it programs nothing.

Some sensors can alternate between two settings of exposure and gain from frame to frame, for HDR. NVIDIA calls that alternating exposure. When it stops, the sensor holds one of the two settings, and no software can tell which.

That is a problem for V4L2. It remembers the last value of every control, and it does not pass a write to the driver when the value did not change. After alternating exposure the remembered value can be wrong. A write of the same number is then dropped, although the sensor holds another setting.

The marker repairs that. A program writes the maximum plus one, and tegracam accepts the value without a call to the driver. The remembered value now differs from every real value, so the next real write reaches the sensor. For a capture with v4l2-ctl one consequence remains: the range in the listing is one larger than the range in the device tree.

sensor_mode selects the mode, when a flag says so

Section 4 defined a mode: one complete setting of the sensor, and one row of the mode list. On the Jetson, two things decide which row is active, and their names are easy to confuse.

sensor_mode is a control. Its value is the number of a row. For the IMX477, 0 is the 3840×2160 mode and 1 is the 1920×1080 mode. Row N of the C table and the node modeN of the device tree describe the same mode, so one number names both.

use_sensor_mode_id is a flag in the device tree. It does not select a list, because both lists are always in use. It selects the way in which a row is chosen:

use_sensor_mode_id How the mode is chosen
"false", the default From the request. The framework finds the row with the requested width and height. NVIDIA's camera software also compares the color format and the frame rate.
"true" From the number in sensor_mode. A requested size that differs from that row is corrected back, and no error is reported.

Both ways exist because a request cannot always name a mode. Two modes can have the same size and the same rate, for example one that bins and one that crops. With the flag, NVIDIA's camera software names the row directly. NVIDIA's driver guide states both cases (see References). The sensor node of the IMX477 sets the flag:

Jetson Linux r39.2.1hardware/nvidia/t23x/nv-public/overlay/tegra234-p3767-camera-p3768-imx477-A.dtslines 114

bypass_mode chooses the path to memory

The last two entries of the listing are not sensor controls. The VI channel registers bypass_mode and override_enable on the video node, because the same /dev/video0 serves two capture paths. When Argus owns the camera, it sets bypass_mode to 1. The frames then go through the RTCPU capture path to the ISP, instead of the VI-to-memory path of this lesson.

The default is 0, so direct V4L2 capture works without configuration. However, an Argus session that ended mid-stream can leave the value at 1. That is why Jetson raw-capture recipes include v4l2-ctl -c bypass_mode=0, and the recipe of section 9 starts with it. This lesson does not use override_enable.

Warning

select timeout or Resource temporarily unavailable on --stream-mmap.

  • Cause: the queue started and no frame arrived. The message states only that no pixels reached VI.

Self-check

Three tasks: capture frames, enumerate the pipeline topology, and read the controls of one stage directly. Which device node serves each task?
Show the answer
  • The frames come through /dev/video0, the only node that transfers pixel data: the video_device of the VI channel with its vb2 queue.
  • The topology comes through /dev/media0, the map of entities, pads and links that the media controller keeps.
  • The controls of one stage come through its /dev/v4l-subdev* node. On the devkit, subdev1 is the IMX477 and subdev0 is the NVCSI channel.
In the log line tegra-camrtc-capture-vi tegra-capture-vi: subdev imx477 9-001a bound, which two drivers connect, and what does the bind provide?
Show the answer
  • The I2C driver of the sensor, which registered the subdev imx477 9-001a, and the VI platform driver tegra-camrtc-capture-vi.
  • The bind links the sensor subdev into the media graph of VI and names a video node after it, vi-output, imx477 9-001a.
  • It is the step that turns a probed sensor into a /dev/videoN that can capture.
On a Raspberry Pi 5, a program starts a capture on the node rp1-cfe-csi2_ch0 directly after boot, and the capture fails. What did the program not do, and why does the same program work on the Jetson?
Show the answer
  • The driver of the front end creates the links to its video nodes without the enabled flag. A program must enable the link that it needs and set a format on each pad.
  • On the Jetson both links are enabled from the start. The driver of /dev/video0 passes a format to the sensor itself.
v4l2-ctl --list-formats-ext against a probed virtcam reports two sizes. Its overlay declares three DT modes, and Argus lists all three. What is the source of each list, and why does the devkit IMX477 not show the same difference?
Show the answer
  • v4l2-ctl enumerates the camera_common_frmfmt[] C table of the driver, which has two rows for virtcam.
  • Argus reads the mode0..modeN nodes of the device tree, and the overlay of virtcam has three.
  • The two consumers read different sources, and the build does not cross-check them. Lesson 08 covers this layer boundary.
  • The stock IMX477 has two modes in its C table and two in its device tree, so its two listings agree.
A captured RG10 frame opened as 16-bit grayscale looks black. What is the arithmetic?
Show the answer
  • RG10 puts each 10-bit sample in the low bits of a 16-bit little-endian word, with the upper six bits zero. That is the T_R16 memory format of VI.
  • The largest sample is 1023 against a 16-bit full scale of 65535, that is 1.6% brightness.
  • A multiplication by 64, a left shift by 6, makes the image visible. In the toy file, 900 becomes 57600.
A simple demosaic looks green. What are the two reasons, and what corrects it in normal operation?
Show the answer
  • Under white light the green sites of the sensor read higher than the red and blue sites. A naive demosaic takes the samples as they are.
  • No white balance has scaled red and blue relative to green.
  • Demosaic and AWB are ISP functions driven by the 3A loops of Argus, and the raw path bypasses the ISP. Lesson 12 uses the ISP path.
With gain_factor = "16" and exposure_factor = "1000000", what do -c gain=64 and -c exposure=8000 set physically?
Show the answer
  • Control value ÷ factor = physical value.
  • Gain 64 ÷ 16 = 4.0× analog gain.
  • Exposure 8000 ÷ 10⁶ s = 8 ms. The device tree gives the exposure limits in microseconds for that reason.
v4l2-ctl -l reports gain: min=16 max=357, and the device tree says max_gain_val = "356". What is the value 357 for?
Show the answer
  • tegracam registers the gain and exposure ranges as the DT maximum plus one.
  • The extra value is a sentinel. It means that a previous alternating-exposure capture left the setting indeterminate. Then the sensor must not be programmed, per the comment above tegracam_init_ctrl_ranges_by_mode().
  • The dispatch of a control write returns 0 without calling the driver when it sees the sentinel. On kernel 6.8 that comparison is guarded by check_add_overflow.
v4l2-ctl --set-fmt-video=width=1920,height=1080 alone keeps producing 3840×2160 frames on this driver. Why, and what is the fix?
Show the answer
  • The device tree sets use_sensor_mode_id = "true", so camera_common_try_fmt takes the mode from the sensor_mode control. It defaults to 0, the 3840×2160 mode.
  • A request that does not match that mode is rewritten to the size of the mode without an error.
  • Fix: run v4l2-ctl -c sensor_mode=1 first, then set the format, and confirm with v4l2-ctl -V.
